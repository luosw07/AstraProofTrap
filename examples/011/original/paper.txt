                                                    THE ARTIN–DAVENPORT CONJECTURE ON CUBIC FORMS

                                                        DANTE BONOLIS, TIM BROWNING, JAKOB GLAS, AND VICTOR Y. WANG


                                                   Abstract. We show that any integral cubic form in ten or more variables has a nontrivial
                                                   integer zero. Like earlier authors on this topic, we use geometric methods to produce a zero
                                                   in some cases, and the circle method in all other cases. New ingredients are required on both
                                                   fronts: in the first case, bounds on the dimension of singular loci and incidence varieties, and in
                                                   the second case, a stratified geometric sieve, together with stratification results for point counts
                                                   over finite fields and a cube-root Hensel lifting analysis.

                                                                                         1. Introduction
                                              In his presidential address to the London Mathematical Society in November 1959, Daven-
                                           port [Dav60] conjectures that any integral cubic form in n ⩾ 10 variables always possesses a
                                           nontrivial integer zero. The exact same conjecture was attributed to Artin by Lang [Lan60],
                                           in his address to the American Mathematical Society in February 1960. For n ⩽ 9, there exist
                                           cubic forms which fail to have nontrivial integer zeros. For example, if K/Q is a cubic number
                                           field with Z-basis ω1 , ω2 , ω3 for the ring of integers oK , and if p is a rational prime that is inert
                                           in oK , then the nonary cubic form
                                                                NK/Q (x1 , x2 , x3 ) + pNK/Q (x4 , x5 , x6 ) + p2 NK/Q (x7 , x8 , x9 )
                                           has only the trivial zero over Qp , where NK/Q (x, y, z) = NK/Q (xω1 + yω2 + zω3 ) is the associated
                                           norm form. Thanks to work of Demyanov [Dem50] (when p ̸= 3) and Lewis [Lew52] (in general),
                                           it has been known since the 1950s that cubic forms in n ⩾ 10 variables have a nontrivial p-adic
                                           zero for every prime p.
                                              In 1959, Davenport [Dav59] adapted the circle method to prove that n ⩾ 32 suffices for any
                                           integral cubic form in n variables to possess a nontrivial integer zero. This was first improved to
                                           n ⩾ 29 in 1962 and then to n ⩾ 16 in 1963 by Davenport [Dav62, Dav63], before being extended
                                           to n ⩾ 14 by Heath-Brown [HB07] in 2007, using an averaged van der Corput variant of the
                                           circle method. The following is our main result.
                                           Theorem 1.1. The Artin–Davenport conjecture is true.
                                              Suppose that X ⊂ Pn−1 is a cubic hypersurface defined by a cubic form F ∈ Z[x1 , . . . , xn ].
                                           The arithmetic of X has been the subject of intense focus over the years, with many results that
                                           work towards the Artin–Davenport conjecture, or that apply in smaller dimensions. Compelling
                                           evidence is provided by Heath-Brown [HB83], who establishes the conjecture for the special class
                                           of smooth cubic hypersurfaces X. It follows from Hooley [Hoo88, Hoo91, Hoo13] that we have
                                           the same conclusion if X has at worst ordinary double points.

                                             2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 11D25 (11G35, 11P55, 11T23, 14D10).
   Assuming that X is geometrically integral, not equal to a cone, and defined over Q, and that
n ⩾ 4, the existence of a single rational point on X is enough to demonstrate the Q-unirationality
of X, and so the Zariski density of X(Q) in X. Variants of this result have been known for many
years, and can be found in the work of Colliot-Thélène and Salberger [CTS89, Proposition 1.3]
and Kollár [Kol02].
   For a large class of possibly singular cubic hypersurfaces X ⊂ Pn−1 , Colliot-Thélène [Bro10,
Appendix] has calculated the Brauer group Br(Y ) associated to a smooth model Y of X. (This
calculation is based on earlier unpublished work of Salberger.) As a consequence of this and
the conjecture of Colliot-Thélène [CT03] that the Brauer–Manin obstruction should control the
Hasse principle for rationally connected varieties, one is led to the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.2. Let X ⊂ Pn−1 be a cubic hypersurface defined over Q which is not a cone,
with n ⩾ 5 and singular locus which is empty or of codimension at least 4 in X. Then the Hasse
principle holds for X.
   This conjecture is formulated in [Bro10, §1]. As a crucial auxiliary result, we shall prove the
following result in Lemma 3.9, which may prove to be of independent interest.
Theorem 1.3. Let n ⩾ 2 and let X ⊂ Pn−1 be a cubic hypersurface defined over Q, with singular
locus of codimension at most ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ in X. Then X(Q) ̸= ∅.
  We note here that ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ = 3 for 10 ⩽ n ⩽ 12 and ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ = 4 for 13 ⩽ n ⩽ 15.

Overview of the paper. Our paper may be read linearly as usual, but the following non-linear
outline of the main ideas may be useful. Associated to a cubic form F ∈ Z[x1 , . . . , xn ] is the n×n
Hessian matrix H(x), which is formed from the second partial derivatives of F . Throughout
this paper, let |x| := max{|x1 |, . . . , |xn |} for x ∈ Rn . We will say that the cubic form F is good
if for any H ⩾ 1 and any ε > 0 we have the upper bound
                          #{x ∈ Zn : |x| ⩽ H, rank H(x) = r} ≪ H r+ε ,
for each integer 0 ⩽ r ⩽ n. A crucial step in Davenport’s [Dav63] treatment of general cubic
forms is a proof of the fact that forms that fail to be good automatically possess nontrivial
integer zeros. In the alternative case when F is good and n ⩾ 16, he shows that
                     N (P ) = #{x ∈ Zn : |x − P x0 | ⩽ P, F (x) = 0} ≫ P n−3                     (1.1)
via the circle method, where x0 ∈ Rn is a suitable nonsingular zero of F . Our basic strategy
follows the same line of attack. However, we go further in that we assume throughout that
X(Q) = ∅. From this we are able to draw various consequences about the geometry of X, such
as Theorem 1.3, which are then used to control the exponential sums involved. More precisely,
we show that if X(Q) = ∅, then (a smoothed version of) N (P ) also satisfies (1.1). This is clearly
a contradiction to X(Q) = ∅, and hence a rational point must have existed from the outset.
   The circle method is introduced in §12. We shall adopt (a smoothed version of) the Klooster-
man circle method approach that forms the backbone of the works by Heath-Brown [HB83] and
Hooley [Hoo88], and which relies on an application of Poisson summation to bring into play the
complete exponential sums

                          Sq (v) :=                  eq (aF (x) + v · x),                     (1.2)
                                    a∈(Z/qZ)∗ x∈(Z/qZ)n

for integers q ⩾ 1 and vectors v ∈ Zn . When q is prime the estimation of these sums in
[HB83, Hoo88] uses the full force of Deligne’s resolution of the Weil conjectures [Del80]. This
is one of several places where tight control over the singular locus of the cubic hypersurface X
enters the argument. Our main idea can be summarised as using the stratification theory of
exponential sums over finite fields as a proxy for the breakdown in control over Sq (v) whenever
X has a large-dimensional singular locus.

handled separately in §8 by a localisation trick.
   Moreover, the integral estimates in §12 tend to control a large shifted box of vectors v at
once, not just a single real point v. An adelic box A of vectors v, on which |Sr (v)| and |Idr (v)|
are controlled, is then fertile ground for stratification methods to take place: bounds via point
counts, sieves, and traces. Most of our cubefull and integral estimates may be fruitfully restricted
to various natural subsets S ⊂ A, giving bounds that are sensitive to the density of S in A.
A notable exception is the Poisson summation based squarefull bound (Lemma 6.7), which is
reasonably strong in short boxes but often weak in small subvarieties thereof. Our work will
require combining different estimates in different ranges.
   In Lemma 3.9 we prove Theorem 1.3 and obtain further information about the singular loci
of our cubic hypersurface X and its hyperplane sections, assuming that X(Q) = ∅. This leads
to useful exponential sum estimates via work of Hooley, Katz, et al., as we recall in §5.
   In §10 we prove the required cube-free estimates in ten variables, using a stratified geometric
sieve, Proposition 2.9. In §11, this is combined with the corresponding ten variable cubefull
estimates of §7. The proof of Theorem 1.1 is then completed in §12 by incorporating the integral
factor, as well as a main term calculation based on work of Bernert [Ber25].

moment argument that is inspired by work of Bonolis–Pierce–Woo [BPW25]. Additionally, by
using projective duality and the theory of singular support, we are able to show in Appendix A
that rational points on Zj actually belong to a subvariety of codimension at least j + 1, under
the assumption X(Q) = ∅.
   Many questions remain open, even for cubic forms in n ⩾ 10 variables over Q. We have been
able to show that for any such cubic form, either geometric methods produce a Q-point, or else
the circle method produces a localised asymptotic formula. It would be interesting to determine
the weakest conditions under which an asymptotic formula can be proven; does goodness suffice?
A related question is that of obtaining lower bounds for point counts and a question of Wooley,
for which we refer to recent work of Kumaraswamy and Rome [KR24].

                                    3. Geometry of cubic forms
   In this section we collect some facts about the geometry of cubic forms over Q. In later
sections, we will be able to spread out much information from Q to finite fields Fp , for all but
finitely many primes p. Let us now introduce the main objects of interest.
   Let F ∈ Q[x1 , . . . , xn ] be a nonzero cubic form in n ⩾ 2 variables. We make no further
assumptions about F , except where specified otherwise. This level of generality will be useful
for a slicing argument in §4. Let

be the Hessian matrix of F , as in §1, formed from the second partial derivatives of F . This gives
a vector-valued bilinear form H(x)y = H(y)x on Qn × Qn .
   Let X ⊂ Pn−1 be the closed subscheme F = 0 over Q. In this section, we work with schemes
in order to avoid edge cases concerning the definition of Sing X, and we work over Q in order

to avoid cumbersome base-change notation. Thus X is a Gal(Q/Q)-stable scheme over Q, and

Remark 3.2. Let us briefly sketch a more conceptual, but less elementary, proof of Lemma 3.1.

Remark 3.7. The content of Lemma 3.6 is best viewed through the lens of geometric invariant
theory (GIT), as we shall now explain. Over Q, the group SLn acts on the space Wn of cubic
forms in n variables via (g · F )(x) = F (g −1 x). A form F is called semistable if the zero form
does not belong to the Zariski closure of the SLn (Q)-orbit of F . Lemma 3.6 is then equivalent to
saying that a cubic form without a nontrivial integer zero is semistable and an alternative proof
of Lemma 3.6 can be obtained using Kempf’s rational version of the geometric Hilbert–Mumford
criterion [Kem78, Corollary 4.3]. Indeed, the elementary proof above is essentially a simplified
version of the proof of this result. See [BMRT13, §4] for related optimality and rationality
results.

and this is reflected in the next set of results, where we draw various conclusions about the
geometry of the singular locus and the Hessian loci associated to our cubic form F . This
connection between GIT stability and singularities has been used before, for example in Laza’s
work [Laz09] on the GIT compactification of the moduli space of cubic fourfolds. Additionally,
a slightly weaker version of Lemma 3.11 was obtained by Park [Par25, Lemma 7.2], also using
semistability.

                                      7. Lifting Smithwork
   As hinted at in §1, the Poisson summation based estimate of §6 is strong for some collections
of moduli q and vectors v. However, for other q and v, it does not fit well with the stratification
estimates that we will provide in §10. To resolve this issue, it will be useful to modify the choice
of the auxiliary lifting modulus A in the factorisation q = A2 T of Lemma 5.7. Very roughly
speaking, in §6 we took A ≈ q 1/2 for a squarefull modulus q, whereas in the present section
we will eventually take A ≈ q 1/3 for a cubefull modulus q. The reduction in the size of A will
slightly weaken our estimates on average over v, while also making the estimates more applicable
to relatively thin sets of vectors v. The restriction to cubefull moduli will also force us to treat
arbitrary cube-free moduli separately later, in §10. The precise manner in which we combine
cube-free and cubefull estimates is somewhat complicated, and is done in §11.
   The main result of this section is Lemma 7.7, concerning cubefull averages for n = 10. In fact,
Lemma 7.7 is restricted to cubefull moduli whose prime factors are all sufficiently large, with
the complementary case being deferred to §8. However, we begin more generally, assuming only
that n ⩾ 10 and X(Q) = ∅, and that q is an arbitrary positive integer. Our results are based
on the fact that the bound for Sq (v) given in (5.14) depends only on v mod A. Thus (5.14)
controls Sq (v) uniformly along a given residue class v mod A.
