                                      § 1. Introduction

   A matching in a hypergraph is a collection of pairwise vertex-disjoint edges. We use weighted
enumeration to determine the mean and typical size of a random matching in a regular hyper-
graph, bound the variance of its size, and obtain large-deviation estimates for the number of
uncovered vertices. For a finite simple hypergraph H, let M(H) denote the set of all matchings,
including the empty matching. For λ > 0, define the matching partition function by

The positive correction nd−1/k gives the scale of the typical deficit from a perfect matching,

Earlier counting results. Many problems in design theory can be expressed as matching
problems in regular hypergraphs. For example, a Steiner system is a perfect matching in the
hypergraph on the t-sets in which each possible block gives an edge consisting of its t-subsets.
Similarly, representing each cell of a Latin square by its row, column and symbol identifies
transversals with perfect matchings in a regular 3-uniform hypergraph. In both examples ar-
bitrary matchings correspond to the respective partial objects. Erdős and Hanani [20] asked
whether, for fixed t < k, one can pack k-sets so that all but a vanishing proportion of the
t-sets are covered, each at most once. Rödl [62] proved this using the nibble. Frankl and
Rödl [23] and Pippenger extended the method to nearly perfect matchings in hypergraphs; see
also Pippenger and Spencer [60]. Quantitative matching bounds were obtained by Alon, Kim
and Spencer [1], Kang, Kühn, Methuku and Osthus [37], and Gould and Kelly [29]. For full

designs, Keevash [38] and Glock, Kühn, Lo and Osthus [28] established existence under the nec-
essary divisibility conditions for sufficiently large orders; Keevash [40] also treated more general
decompositions, including resolvable designs. These results concern the construction of large
matchings; enumeration additionally requires control of the number of choices throughout the
construction.
   For graphs, Schrijver [65] proved a lower bound on the number of perfect matchings in regular
bipartite graphs. For lower bounds on the number of matchings of prescribed size in bipartite
graphs, see [15, 48]. Carroll, Galvin and Tetali [11] and Ilinca and Kahn [32] obtained entropy
bounds for matchings of prescribed size, while Davies, Jenssen, Perkins and Roberts [17] proved
sharp bounds for the matching partition function. See also [18] for sharp coefficient bounds
obtained from stability of the occupancy estimates. For hypergraphs, Asratian and Kuzjurin [2]
counted nearly perfect matchings in almost regular hypergraphs with small codegrees. Related
entropy estimates appear in [16, 45, 51]. The theory of matchings avoiding prescribed forbidden
submatchings was developed by Delcourt and Postle [19] and by Glock, Joos, Kim, Kühn and
Lichev [27]. The latter authors also proved a counting lower bound [27, Theorem 3.5]; with no

prescribing their size, and van der Hofstad, Pendavingh and van der Pol [70] obtained sharper
estimates in several cases. We compare these counts with ours in Section 2.2.

Theorem 1.6 would have consequences for quantitative maximum-matching problems. Indeed,

pair-codegree gap is discussed in Section 9, under “The pair-codegree range”, and the precise

1.2. Proof overview. We first prove the weighted counting formula and then deduce the
matching statistics. The main difficulty is to determine the correction n(λd)−1/k with an error
smaller than this quantity. Our proof combines a new greedy counting argument based on
regular completions with an entropy method based on the intersection patterns introduced
above.
   We begin with Theorem 4.1, proved in Section 4, which gives a lower bound for ZH (λ) uni-
formly over a polynomial range of activities. Each greedy stage stops when half of its vertices
remain, so the degree estimate of Bennett and Bohman [5] is used only over a short interval. We
also control the higher codegrees by applying Freedman’s inequality after dividing each codegree
process by its deterministic decay factor. We count the resulting matching prefixes, assign each
prefix P the weight λ|P | , and sum over all matching extensions, accounting for the different
prefixes contained in the same matching. We then complete each residual hypergraph to an
             MATCHING STATISTICS IN REGULAR HYPERGRAPHS AND APPLICATIONS                        9

exactly regular one and repeat, using an encoding to control the cost of transferring weighted
counts through the completion. The key point is that, after subtracting the leading term and
dividing by the number of vertices, the remainder of the next stage enters with coefficient
1/2 + o(1). Consequently, errors arising later in the iteration receive geometrically decreasing
weights. Together with the higher-codegree estimates, this bounds the total logarithmic loss by
O(nΓ(H)(log n)L ), which is smaller than the desired correction under the hypotheses of Theo-
rem 1.6. This preliminary estimate may be of independent interest: it holds over a polynomial
activity range without assumptions on intersection patterns. In particular, we can apply it at
activity λd, giving the source count needed for the thinning argument below.
  To obtain the positive correction, sample a Gibbs matching M at activity λd and delete each
edge independently with an appropriate probability θ, leaving a matching J. Writing H for
entropy, the entropy chain rule gives

                           H(J) = H(M ) + E[|M |]h(θ) − H(M | J),

where h is the binary entropy function. The middle term records the deletion choices, and the
last term accounts for different original matchings producing the same J. The Gibbs variational
inequality bounds log ZH (λ) from below by H(J) + E[|J|] log λ. Thus, a reconstruction bound
for H(M | J) converts the deletion choices into a weighted counting gain.
   Our intersection patterns control this reconstruction. After specifying which vertices were
already uncovered in M , the deleted edges form a perfect matching on the newly uncovered
vertices. An alternative edge meeting m distinct edges of M is available only when all m are
deleted, an event of probability θm . Its pattern records the sizes of these intersections: the
number of parts determines this probability, while their sizes determine which codegrees bound
the number of alternatives. This records the dependence between vertices of the same matching
edge. We expose the matching in a random order, following Radhakrishnan’s entropy proof of
the Bregman–Minc bound on permanents [61] and its use by Linial and Luria for Steiner triple
systems [49] and by Luria for hypergraph perfect matchings [51]. Here the coefficients bπ (H)
bound the number of alternatives after averaging over vertices and source matchings; integrating
the logarithm of this bound estimates the conditional entropy of the deleted matching.
   The source matching may already leave vertices uncovered, and its average vacancy proba-
bility is unknown. The higher activity contributes a term proportional to the number of these
original vacancies. A tangent-line bound for binary entropy uses this term to control the en-
tropy of distinguishing original vacancies from vertices uncovered by deletion. The resulting
lower bound is uniform in this unknown average vacancy probability. Later, we will show that
choosing θ = (λd)−1/k gives the correction n(λd)−1/k .
   For the upper bound, we describe a Gibbs matching by its uncovered set and a perfect
matching on the remaining vertices. The same random-order exposure and pattern estimates
bound the available matching edges. The coefficient one requires retaining the degree loss caused
by uncovered vertices. Several vacancies in one edge make these losses overlap. We express the
degree loss in terms of the number of edges meeting the uncovered set. Shearer’s inequality and
the Gibbs variational inequality then bound the entropy of that set together with this loss, one
edge at a time. Choosing the auxiliary weights in terms of (λd)−1/k makes the terms involving
the mean vacancy density cancel. Summing the resulting edge estimates gives the correction
n(λd)−1/k , with a Taylor error of order n(λd)−2/k .
10                                        HYUNWOO LEE

   Finally, changes of activity express the exponential moments of the vacancy count as ratios of
partition functions. The counting estimate then gives tail bounds and L2 convergence, yielding
both the sharp mean and concentration. Concentration under the corresponding tilted laws
gives the large-deviation lower bounds.

                                       § 2. Applications

    Our main results give refined counting formulae and typical-size estimates for several fam-
ilies of combinatorial objects. We illustrate these applications with partial Latin squares and
transversals, partial toroidal queens configurations, matchings in Steiner systems, partial de-
signs over sets and vector spaces, and matroids of fixed rank. All objects and their underlying
points are labelled. For partial objects, unless a host is explicitly fixed, the uniform measure is
taken over all valid collections, including the empty collection, without prescribing their size.
The proofs are collected in Section 8.
    We use N, K, D for the order, uniformity and degree of an auxiliary hypergraph H, and all
structural parameters are fixed as the underlying size tends to infinity. The partial objects and
matchings in fixed designs are encoded bijectively as matchings in H, preserving their counts
and uniform measures. The auxiliary degrees are at least a fixed positive power of the order,
so checking the codegrees and intersection coefficients allows us to apply Theorems 1.6 and 1.7.
For matroids, the bijection applies to sparse paving matroids; additional counting comparisons
give the result for paving and arbitrary matroids. The typical-size conclusions below are stated
as L2 convergence. They therefore give the expectation asymptotic and show that the variance
is of smaller order than the square of the typical deficit. In the leading term, we replace the
exact auxiliary degree by an asymptotic equivalent only when the resulting error is o(N D−1/K ).
For the families encoded bijectively as matchings, Theorem 1.7(b) also gives large deviations
for the number of uncovered auxiliary vertices. If the deficit is |U (H)|/K, the logarithmic tail
estimate has leading term −N D−1/K (x log x − x + 1).

0 ≤ ρ(B) ≤ |B|, monotonicity, and

                             ρ(B ∪ C) + ρ(B ∩ C) ≤ ρ(B) + ρ(C).

such partial system specifies a unique sparse paving matroid; see [59, 70].

3.1. Partition functions, the Gibbs measure and fluctuations. A matching can be
viewed as a collection of particles, each occupying the vertices of one edge. The matching
condition is a hard-core exclusion rule: two particles cannot occupy the same vertex. On a
graph, the occupied edges are called dimers and the uncovered vertices monomers. This is the
monomer-dimer model studied by Heilmann and Lieb [31]. The same interpretation applies to
a k-uniform hypergraph, with each particle occupying k vertices.

a configuration M has weight λ|M | . The Gibbs measure is obtained by dividing each weight
by the sum of all weights, the partition function ZH (λ). This is a grand-canonical description,
meaning that the number of particles is allowed to vary.

   The random greedy matching process starts with the empty matching. At each step, it adds
a uniformly chosen edge of the residual hypergraph and deletes the chosen vertices and all
incident edges, stopping when no edges remain. A prefix is the matching formed by an initial
segment of this process, with the order of its edges forgotten. For classical analyses of this
process, see [63, 67].
   The difficulty is to keep the loss in the leading count below the correction nd−1/k needed later.
In a single long trajectory, the available degree error in the estimates of Bennett and Bohman [5]
becomes comparable with the decreasing residual degree too early to give this precision. For
linear 3-uniform hypergraphs, the resulting cutoff is d−1/4 up to logarithmic factors, larger than
the relevant vacancy scale d−1/3 . Regularization with codegree control has precedents in Kang,
Kühn, Methuku and Osthus [37, Lemma 8.1] and Gould and Kelly [29, Lemma 4.1]. Gould
and Kelly also iterate nibble stages while tracking higher codegrees [29, Sections 7–8]. The
new ingredient here is an iteration of weighted counting inequalities through exactly regular
completions. Each stage stops when half the vertices remain; we control the higher codegrees
and restore exact regularity before starting the next stage. This allows the existing degree
estimate to be applied afresh at the current degree scale. An encoding bounds the cost of
transferring weighted counts from the completion to the residual hypergraph. After subtracting
the leading term and dividing by the number of vertices, the next-stage remainder enters with
coefficient 1/2 + o(1), reflecting the proportion of vertices retained in that stage. The resulting
geometric weights, together with the higher-codegree estimates, control the accumulated error
on the finer scale required here. Theorem 4.1 may also be of independent interest: it provides
an explicit codegree error uniformly up to polynomially large activities, without assumptions
on intersection patterns.
   The accumulated error is governed by the full codegree parameter Γ(H) defined in (1.3).
The j = 3 term is bounded by the first term. Since H is nonempty and simple, ∆k = 1 and
Γ(H) ≥ d−1/(k−1) .

                                   § 5. Intersection patterns

   We now develop the entropy bounds that convert the count in Theorem 4.1 into the correction
in Theorem 1.6. Throughout this section, H satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 1.6. All o(1)
terms tend to zero uniformly over these hypergraphs as n → ∞, with k, ε, C fixed; the estimates
for random matchings are also uniform over their laws. For the lower bound, delete each edge of
a random source matching M independently with probability θ, leaving J. The entropy chain
rule gives
                           H(J) = H(M ) + E[|M |]h(θ) − H(M | J).
The term H(M | J) accounts for different sources producing the same J; bounding it allows us
to use the entropy gained by deletion.
   There are two reconstruction problems. First, specify the set W of vertices not covered by J
which were already uncovered in the source. The other vertices form the deleted set S. Once S is
known, reconstructing the source amounts to choosing a perfect matching on H[S]. Intersection
patterns control the choices in this second problem. They record which whole source blocks
must be deleted for an alternative edge to lie in S. The vertices of a source edge are deleted
together, so a vertex-by-vertex independence estimate would miss these correlations. Figure 2
illustrates the two reconstruction problems.
   For the upper count, we first specify the uncovered set and then expose a perfect matching of
the remaining vertices. An alternative is available only if the matching blocks it meets have not
yet been exposed. The same pattern estimates therefore bound the entropy in both arguments.

8.1. Latin squares and toroidal queens.
Proof of Corollary 2.1. Let R, C, S be disjoint sets of n row, column and symbol labels. The
auxiliary hypergraph H has vertex set (R×C)∪(R×S)∪(C ×S). Each triple (i, j, a) ∈ R×C ×S
gives the edge {(i, j), (i, a), (j, a)}, representing symbol a in cell (i, j). Two such edges meet
exactly when their entries repeat a cell, a symbol in a row, or a symbol in a column. Thus
matchings in H are in bijection with Pn . The hypergraph is linear, 3-uniform and n-regular on
3n2 vertices. Its degree is a fixed positive power of its order, and

                                             |U(H)| = 3(n2 − |P |).
              MATCHING STATISTICS IN REGULAR HYPERGRAPHS AND APPLICATIONS                           49

Theorem 1.1 at activity one gives the count, and Theorem 1.7 gives the L2 conclusion. The
expectation and variance estimates follow from this convergence. Row and column permutations
act transitively on the cells and preserve the uniform measure, so each cell is empty with
probability E[n2 − |P |]/n2 ∼ n−1/3 . This completes the proof.                           □
Proof of Corollary 2.2. Use three disjoint sets of n vertices for the rows, columns and symbols,
and associate to each cell (i, j) the edge consisting of its row, column and symbol L(i, j). The
resulting auxiliary hypergraph H is n-regular, linear and 3-uniform on 3n vertices. Its matchings
are in bijection with T (L), and |U(H)| = 3(n − |T |). Theorem 1.1 at activity one gives the
count, and Theorem 1.7 gives the L2 conclusion. For a group multiplication table, the maps
(x, y, xy) 7→ (ax, yb, axyb) act transitively on each of the three vertex classes. Every matching
covers equally many vertices in the three classes, so all their vacancy probabilities agree and
equal E[|U(H)|]/(3n) ∼ n−1/3 . This completes the proof.                                       □
Proof of Corollary 2.3. Use four disjoint copies of Zn for the rows, columns and two types of
diagonals. Each cell gives an edge through its four indices. The resulting auxiliary hypergraph
H is n-regular and 4-uniform on 4n vertices. Its maximum pair codegree is at most two:
specifying the two diagonal indices leaves at most two solutions for a cell, and every other pair
of compatible indices determines at most one cell. For all sufficiently large n, the pair-codegree
bound satisfies ∆2 ≤ n1/8 . The degree is polynomial in the order, so Corollary 1.8 with γ = 1/8
gives the counting estimate and the statistical conclusion. Since |U (H)| = 4(n − Q), the result
follows. Translations of the board act transitively on each row, column and diagonal class. Since
each matching occupies equally many vertices in each class, every vacancy probability equals
E[|U(H)|]/(4n) ∼ n−1/4 , for odd and even n alike. This completes the proof.                    □

on higher codegrees and intersection patterns. The weighted estimate yields the sharp mean
vacancy, concentration and large deviations, and gives refined counts in the applications, includ-
ing matroids of each fixed rank at least four. The main remaining questions concern sharper
fluctuations, weaker hypotheses and the mixed hyperplane sizes in rank three.
